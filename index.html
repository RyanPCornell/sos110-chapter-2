2.1 · How Do Ethics Influence Our Decisions?

Personal Ethics

A set of moral principles guiding our sense of right and wrong — shaped by personal beliefs and values.

© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.1 · Duties and Rights

Ethics

Duties & Rights
Ethical systems rooted in obligations (duty to care) and entitlements (right to a healthy environment). Example: humans have a moral and legal duty to care for dependents; children have a right to adequate care.
Intrinsic Value
The worth of something in and of itself — independent of human utility. Used in deontological ethical reasoning; basis for the 1970 Environmental Policy Act.
UN Earth Charter (2000)
Outlines global environmental duties, including the protection and restoration of Earth's ecological systems — a landmark deontological document in international environmental ethics.
Environmental Ethics
How should we examine the moral relationships between humans and the environment?
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.1 · The Greatest Good for the Greatest Number

Utilitarianism

An ethical framework that defines “right” as whichever action produces the most benefit (or usefulness to humans) for the greatest number of people. The outcome carries more moral weight than the means.

Calculation
Total benefits and harms experienced by all affected parties are weighed; the option with the highest net benefit is pursued. Utilitarian value is value based on usefulness to humans.
Trade-off Example
A hydroelectric dam may power underserved regions but flood local homes. Utilitarianism asks: does the total benefit justify the harm?
Environmental Use
Governments use utilitarian thinking for cost-benefit analyses of regulations, infrastructure, and resource management decisions.

Key tension: utilitarianism can justify harming a minority for majority benefit. Environmental justice advocates argue that distributional fairness must also be weighed — not just aggregate outcomes.

© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.2 · Supply, Demand, and Markets

Global Oil Prices, Shaped by Supply and Demand

Demand
How much people are willing and able to pay for a good at a given price.
Supply
How much of a good is available for sale at a given price in a market.
Market
A system that brings buyers and sellers together. Adam Smith (1776) described the ideal market as an “invisible hand” — when goods grow scarce, prices rise, signaling producers.

Fossil fuels exemplify supply-demand dynamics: rising demand + limited supply → higher prices, shifting incentives toward alternatives.

2.2 · Efficiency Gains Can Inspire Increased Consumption

The Jevons Paradox

Efficiency gains in resource use lower the cost of that resource — which tends to increase consumption. Named for economist William Jevons, who first observed this in 19th-century coal use.

  • A more fuel-efficient car lowers the per-mile cost of driving, encouraging people to drive more.

Implication: technological efficiency alone cannot guarantee reduced environmental impact without behavioral or policy changes.

© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.3 · How Can Economics Help Us Understand Environmental Problems?

Externalities

Costs or benefits of economic activity that are not reflected in the market price of a good or service. When externalities are ignored, markets systematically under- or over-produce.

Negative Externality
Health costs from coal-plant emissions fall on communities, not producers. These costs are excluded from the market price of coal.
Positive Externality
Clean water and biodiversity provided by intact forests benefit society but generate no income for the forest — so markets tend to under-protect them.

2.3 · Tragedy of the Commons

What Is the Tragedy of the Commons?

When a shared resource is used by individuals acting in their own short-term self-interest, overexploitation degrades or destroys the resource for everyone.

  • Each user gains full benefit from additional use but shares the cost of degradation with all others.
  • Benefits are internalized, while costs are externalized.
  • It is a collective action problem.
  • Collective action is the coordinated efforts of groups to achieve common goals.
  • Without coordination among commons users, the rational individual choice leads to collective ruin.
  • Examples: overfishing, groundwater depletion, atmospheric CO₂ emissions.
  • Solutions include regulation, privatization of the resource, coordination and collective action, or community-based governance (Ostrom, 1990).

Ostrom, E. (1990). Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action. Cambridge University Press.

2.3 · Positive Externalities, Ecosystem Services & Public Goods

When Markets Under-Produce Good Things

Positive Externalities
Benefits of economic activity not captured in market prices. Functioning ecosystems provide clean water, air, soil fertility, and aesthetic value — without compensation.
Ecosystem Valuation
Assigns dollar values to the range of benefits that ecosystem services provide people/society. Markets underproduce these because producers cannot capture the value.
Public Goods
Things that cannot be produced profitably because non-paying users also benefit (non-excludable, non-rival). Roads and parks are examples of public goods the government provides through tax revenue.

2.3 · How Governments Address Negative Externalities

Three Policy Approaches

Limits / Standards
Government-mandated caps on emissions or pollution levels.
Taxes / Fees
A tax on each unit of pollution makes negative externalities visible in market prices, incentivizing producers to reduce harm.
Cap-and-Trade
Permits can be bought, sold, traded, or banked — harnessing market forces to achieve environmental goals.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.4 · Why Do We Have Environmental Policy?

Policies Guide Behavior

Policies are authoritative decisions — laws, regulations, court rulings — that guide the behavior of individuals and organizations.

  • Personal choices (transit, cars, energy) are shaped by funding policy, safety standards, and trade rules.
  • Policy is made through political processes; elections at every level carry environmental consequences.
  • The policy cycle: Identify problem → Craft policy → Approve policy → Implement policy → Evaluate policy — and the cycle repeats.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.4 · Global Environmental Politics

Environmental Problems Cross Borders

  • Example: Acid rain in Canada can originate from coal plants in the United States, pollution ignores political boundaries.
  • Collaboration: Intergovernmental Organizations (IGOs) like the UN (193 members) create venues for international collaboration (important for issues that involve multiple perspectives and stakeholders).
