2.1 · How Do Ethics Influence Our Decisions?

Personal Ethics

A set of moral principles guiding our sense of right and wrong — shaped by personal beliefs and values.

  • Ethical judgments combine personal beliefs with broader values about fairness, harm, and responsibility.
  • Case — BPA: In 2010, the FDA reported that bisphenol A in plastics (including baby bottles) is hazardous. Consumer ethics drove demand for BPA-free products and campaigns to ban them.
  • Ethics enters environmental decision-making when we ask: Who bears the harm? Who benefits? What do we owe future generations?
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.1 · Duties and Rights

Deontological Ethics

Duties & Rights
Ethical systems rooted in obligations (duty to care) and entitlements (right to a healthy environment). Example: humans have a moral and legal duty to care for dependents; children have a right to adequate care.
Intrinsic Value
The worth of something in and of itself — independent of human utility. Used in deontological ethical reasoning; basis for the 1970 Environmental Policy Act.
UN Earth Charter (2000)
Outlines global environmental duties, including the protection and restoration of Earth's ecological systems — a landmark deontological document in international environmental ethics.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.1 · The Greatest Good for the Greatest Number

Utilitarianism

An ethical framework that defines “right” as whichever action produces the most benefit (or least harm) for the greatest number of people. The outcome carries more moral weight than the means.

Calculation
Total benefits and harms experienced by all affected parties are weighed; the option with the highest net benefit is pursued.
Trade-off Example
A hydroelectric dam may power underserved regions but flood local homes. Utilitarianism asks: does the total benefit justify the harm?
Environmental Use
Governments use utilitarian thinking for cost-benefit analyses of regulations, infrastructure, and resource management decisions.

Key tension: utilitarianism can justify harming a minority for majority benefit. Environmental justice advocates argue that distributional fairness must also be weighed — not just aggregate outcomes.

2.1 · Who or What Do We Care About?

Ethical Boundaries

Anthropocentrism
Draws ethical boundaries around human interests. Environmental harm matters only insofar as it affects people.
Biocentrism
Extends concern to all living things (e.g., endangered species). Championed by animal rights advocates.
Ecocentrism
Protects both living and nonliving components of ecosystems (Aldo Leopold). Aligns with indigenous wisdom: “When we take care of nature, nature takes care of us” (Jessica Hernandez).

2.2 · How Are the Environment and Economy Connected?

Economic Systems & Natural Resources

Every purchase connects us to an economic system that shapes the production, distribution, and consumption of goods — and draws on natural resources at every stage.

1 · Raw Materials
Extraction and transport of natural resources (minerals, timber, fuels).
2 · Production
Equipment, energy, and labor convert materials into goods.
3 · Distribution
Transport networks move goods to markets.
4 · Consumption
Purchasing decisions aggregate into demand signals that drive the cycle.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.2 · Supply, Demand, and Markets

Global Oil Prices, Shaped by Supply and Demand

Demand
How much people are willing and able to pay for a good at a given price.
Supply
How much of a good is available for sale at a given price in a market.
Market
A system that brings buyers and sellers together. Adam Smith (1776) described the ideal market as an “invisible hand” — when goods grow scarce, prices rise, signaling producers.

Fossil fuels exemplify supply-demand dynamics: rising demand + limited supply → higher prices, shifting incentives toward alternatives.

© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.2 · Efficiency Gains Can Inspire Increased Consumption

The Jevons Paradox

Efficiency gains in resource use lower the cost of that resource — which tends to increase consumption. Named for economist William Jevons, who first observed this in 19th-century coal use.

  • A more fuel-efficient car lowers the per-mile cost of driving, encouraging people to drive more.

Implication: technological efficiency alone cannot guarantee reduced environmental impact without behavioral or policy changes.

© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.3 · How Can Economics Help Us Understand Environmental Problems?

Externalities

Costs or benefits of economic activity that are not reflected in the market price of a good or service. When externalities are ignored, markets systematically under- or over-produce.

Negative Externality
Health costs from coal-plant emissions fall on communities, not producers. These costs are excluded from the market price of coal.
Positive Externality
Clean water and biodiversity provided by intact forests benefit society but generate no income for the forest — so markets tend to under-protect them.

2.3 · How Governments Address Negative Externalities

Three Policy Approaches

Limits / Standards
Government-mandated caps on emissions or pollution levels.
Taxes / Fees
A tax on each unit of pollution makes negative externalities visible in market prices, incentivizing producers to reduce harm.
Cap-and-Trade
Permits can be bought, sold, traded, or banked — harnessing market forces to achieve environmental goals.

2.3 · Tragedy of the Commons

What Is the Tragedy of the Commons?

When a shared resource is used by individuals acting in their own short-term self-interest, overexploitation degrades or destroys the resource for everyone.

  • Each user gains full benefit from additional use but shares the cost of degradation with all others.
  • Benefits are internalized, while costs are externalized.
  • Without coordination among commons users, the rational individual choice leads to collective ruin.
  • Examples: overfishing, groundwater depletion, atmospheric CO₂ emissions.
  • Solutions include regulation, privatization of the resource, coordination, or community-based governance (Ostrom, 1990).

2.3 · Positive Externalities, Ecosystem Services & Public Goods

When Markets Under-Produce Good Things

Positive Externalities
Benefits of economic activity not captured in market prices. Functioning ecosystems provide clean water, air, soil fertility, and aesthetic value — without compensation.
Ecosystem Services
Assigns dollar values to the range of benefits from naturally functioning ecosystems. Markets underproduce these because producers cannot capture the value.
Public Goods
Things that cannot be produced profitably because non-paying users also benefit (non-excludable, non-rival). Roads and parks are examples of public goods the government provides through tax revenue.

2.4 · Why Do We Have Environmental Policy?

Policies Guide Behavior

Policies are authoritative decisions — laws, regulations, court rulings — that guide the behavior of individuals and organizations.

  • Personal choices (transit, cars, energy) are shaped by funding policy, safety standards, and trade rules.
  • Policy is made through political processes; elections at every level carry environmental consequences.
  • The policy cycle: Identify problem → Craft policy → Approve policy → Implement policy → Evaluate policy — and the cycle repeats.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.4 · Environmental Policy in the United States

A Century of US Environmental Policy

Natural Resource Policy (late 1800s)
Industrialization prompted conservation interest. Yellowstone became the world's first national park; national forests were protected.
Key Land-Use Federal Agencies (1900s)
National Park Service, US Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, and US Fish & Wildlife Service established to manage public lands.
Pollution Control Legislation (1970s)
Clean Water Act, Clean Air Act, and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) created to address industrial pollution and public health.
Conservation Leaders
Gifford Pinchot (utilitarian conservation) and John Muir (preservation) shaped early 20th-century policy alongside President Theodore Roosevelt.

2.4 · State and Local Policies

Federalism in Environmental Governance

The federal government sets minimum environmental standards; states are responsible for meeting them through their own policies — and can exceed federal standards.

  • California sets more stringent air and emissions standards than federal law — and other states may adopt California's tougher rules.
  • Texas agencies (Texas Commission on Environmental Quality, Public Utility Commission, Texas Parks & Wildlife) oversee a wide range of state-level environmental policies.
  • State and local elections carry significant environmental weight — local officials set land use, water, and waste policies that directly affect daily life.

Federal minimum standards create a floor; state innovation and political will determine the ceiling of environmental protection.

© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
2.4 · Global Environmental Politics

Environmental Problems Cross Borders

  • Acid rain in Canada can originate from coal plants in the United States — pollution ignores political boundaries.
  • Citizens in China and India recycle toxic e-waste generated by Western consumption patterns.
  • Demand for ivory and shark fins in one country drives species decline in another.
  • Intergovernmental Organizations (IGOs) like the UN (193 members) create venues for international collaboration.
  • Since the 1970s, international agreements have addressed ozone depletion, biodiversity, and climate change.

2.5 · What Can I Do?

Your Consumer Choices Have Power

Purchase Intentionally
Support products and businesses that promote environmental health. Apps like Good Guide provide ratings on the environmental practices of manufacturers.
Boycott
Withhold buying power from companies to motivate change — collective consumer action has driven corporate sustainability policy shifts.
Vote & Advocate
Support officials who prioritize environmental protection. Mobilize voters, attend meetings, run for office, or join a campaign.
Engage Elected Officials
Contact Congress and local officials (email, phone, letters). Organize rallies or join advocacy organizations. Visit USA.gov for contact information.
