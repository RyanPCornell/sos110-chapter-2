Methodology — How This Calculator Works▾
This is one valuation framework, not the true cost

The calculator uses damage-cost valuation: it estimates the physical harm caused by an activity, then converts that harm into present-day dollars. That is a legitimate and widely used method — it underpins EPA regulatory analysis — but it is a choice, not a measurement, and it carries ethical commitments that reasonable people reject.

Three of those commitments do most of the work here:

AssumptionWhy it is contested
Future harm is discountedMoving only the discount rate from 2.0% to 1.5% raises the SCC from $190 to $340 — nearly doubling every climate line in this tool — with no change whatsoever in the underlying physics. No scientific finding selects that rate; it is a judgement about how much a person born in 2100 counts relative to one alive now.
Harm can be priced at allAssigning dollars to a lost species, a displaced community, or a premature death treats them as commensurable with money. Rights-based (deontological) ethics holds that some harms are wrongs to be prohibited, not costs to be traded off — so on that view the entire exercise is category error.
Aggregate totals are the right unitA single national average conceals who actually bears the harm. Air pollution and extraction damages fall disproportionately on low-income communities and communities of colour; a per-person figure is silent about that distribution.

Read the headline number as “one defensible estimate under stated assumptions,” not as the cost. A different-but-equally-defensible set of choices would produce a materially different figure — which is exactly what the range beside the total shows.

Uncertainty is large, and the range is a scenario, not a confidence interval

The figure beside the total is built by moving every parameter to its low end simultaneously, then to its high end — SCC, fuel economy, radiative forcing, diet study, air-quality damages, per-item consumer rates. That is deliberately a bounding exercise.

Because the parameters are moved together, the low and high ends assume the uncertainties are perfectly correlated, which they are not. Treat them as plausible bounding scenarios, not as a 95% confidence interval, and do not read the width as a probability statement. A genuine interval would require a Monte Carlo treatment with correlations between parameters — beyond what this teaching tool attempts.

These are average, not marginal, externalities

Every coefficient here is an average lifecycle intensity. The result therefore estimates the externality embodied in a pattern of living — not the social benefit of avoiding one more unit of anything.

The distinction bites hardest for electricity. This tool applies the average grid mix you select, but the generator that actually responds when you add or remove a kilowatt-hour is the marginal one — frequently natural gas, and in some hours and regions considerably dirtier or cleaner than the average. Avoided emissions from switching off a light are not the average rate shown. The same gap applies to a single flight (load factors, aircraft substitution, whether the flight departs regardless) and to a single garment.

So do not read a line item as a saving. “Flights (long) $246” does not mean society gains $246 if you cancel one flight. It means: of the total externality embodied in this lifestyle, $246 is attributable to long-haul flying.

Where these numbers apply

The model is US-centred. Emission factors (EPA), energy use (EIA), air-quality damages (NAS/APEEP), transit intensity (FTA), spending (BEA) and home size (US Census) are all US figures, and the defaults describe a US household.

Some inputs are global: food externalities (FAO), e-waste (Forti et al.), streaming infrastructure (IEA) and aviation radiative forcing (IPCC). Mixing the two is a real limitation — a global average food footprint applied to a US diet will misstate some streams.

The SCC is deliberately global: a tonne of CO₂ does the same damage wherever it is emitted, and EPA's $190 counts worldwide damages. One consequence worth stating plainly is that most of the climate harm counted here does not fall on the person generating it. Air-quality damages behave in the opposite way — they are intensely local, and the same tailpipe emission harms far more people in a dense city than in a rural county. The fixed $/mile rates average over that variation entirely.

The inputs are treated as independent, but they are not

Each slider moves on its own. In reality household electricity, floor area, consumer spending, device purchases and vehicle miles are strongly correlated — larger, higher-income households tend to be higher on all of them at once.

Two consequences: the tool will let you build combinations that rarely occur together (a 4,000 sq ft home with minimal consumption), and it cannot show you the compounding that makes real high-consumption lifestyles diverge from averages faster than any single slider suggests.

The Social Cost of Carbon (SCC) is the single most important parameter in this calculator. It converts every tonne of CO₂ equivalent emitted into a dollar cost representing the present value of all future damages that emission causes — sea-level rise, extreme weather, agricultural disruption, ecosystem loss, and human mortality from heat stress.

ScenarioSCC ValueDiscount RateSource & Context
Low estimate$51/tCO₂e3%Interagency Working Group interim estimate (2021), restoring the pre-2017 methodology. Now widely considered too low.
Central (used here)$190/tCO₂e2.0%EPA (2023) revised estimate. Uses updated damage functions, lower near-term Ramsey discount rate, and comprehensive global impact accounting. (EPA's full range: $120 at 2.5% to $340 at 1.5%.)
High estimate$308/tCO₂e1.5%Rennert et al. (2022), Nature — central estimate $185 at 2%; $308 at a 1.5% discount rate. Comprehensive evidence including mortality, agriculture, energy, and coastal damages.

How the SCC is applied: For every activity that produces CO₂ equivalent emissions, the calculator multiplies kg of CO₂e by (SCC ÷ 1,000) to get dollars. For example: driving a gas car 12,000 miles at 0.355 kg CO₂/mile produces 4,260 kg = 4.26 tonnes CO₂. At $190/tonne: 4.26 × $190 = $809 in climate damages. Air quality and other externalities are added separately using NAS (2010) rates.

All consumer externalities include supply-chain impacts across the full lifecycle — not just point-of-sale or use-phase impacts. This is the key distinction between this calculator's approach and simple carbon footprint calculators that only count direct emissions.

System boundary — avoiding double-counting: Streaming and AI use both consume electricity in two places: on your device at home, and in off-site data centres and network infrastructure. The home-side electricity is already captured by the Annual Electricity slider in Home Energy, so the Streaming and AI line items count only the off-site (upstream) portion. This is also why those two items use US-average grid rates rather than your selected home grid — Netflix's and OpenAI's data centres don't run on your utility plan. Drawing system boundaries carefully like this is a core practice in lifecycle assessment.

Not every coefficient in this tool has the same standing. Some are lifted directly from a published table; some are computed from published values by a stated formula; and some are calibration choices — judgement anchored on the literature, but not a number any single paper reports. Conflating the three would overstate the model's precision, so every input is tagged below.

Derivation: the diet table

FAO's True Cost of Food (2021) reports global agrifood externalities decomposed into environmental, social and health streams; True Price (2022) reports per-product external costs for a set of foods. Neither publishes “dollars per person per year by diet type,” which is what this tool needs. The table was built in three steps:

1. Adopt FAO's four-stream structure (climate, water, land, health) so each stream traces to a distinct damage literature rather than one blended figure. 2. Anchor the mixed omnivore row — the US-typical diet — at the per-capita magnitude implied by FAO's national-scale figures. 3. Scale the other three diets by the relative footprints reported in the product-level literature, principally Poore & Nemecek (2018), whose meta-analysis gives the ratio between animal- and plant-heavy diets for land, water and emissions.

Step 3 is where the judgement concentrates. The ratios between diets are far better established than the absolute levels, so comparisons across diet types in this tool are more reliable than any single diet's dollar figure. The Sensitivity Analysis varies the whole table by ×0.62 to ×1.55 to reflect the spread across published studies.

Derivation: the spending multiplier

The general-shopping term is $340 × multiplier, covering supply-chain and end-of-life externalities of goods not itemised elsewhere. Both parts are calibration choices:

The $340 base is set as the residual once clothing, electronics, food and energy are removed from economy-wide per-capita consumption externality estimates (True Price 2022; Trucost/TEEB 2013), so that itemised and non-itemised consumption do not overlap. The multipliers (0.45 / 1.0 / 1.8 / 3.2) bracket consumption tiers roughly spanning the spread between lower and upper US expenditure quintiles in BEA personal consumption data — they are not a fitted function of income, and no equation maps a dollar of spending onto a multiplier.

Treat this line as indicative rather than estimated. If you need one number from this tool to be defensible, do not choose this one. It exists so that consumption beyond the itemised categories is not silently valued at zero — an omission that would bias the total downward more than a rough estimate does.

Overlap: how double-counting was checked

The method is a single rule: each physical harm is assigned to exactly one input. Where two inputs could plausibly claim the same harm, one is scoped down. Every overlap identified is listed here, including the one that required a correction.

Potential overlapResolution
Streaming ↔ home electricityWas double-counted; corrected. Streaming originally charged the full ~65g CO₂/hr chain, but 72% of that is device electricity already metered by the Home Energy slider. Streaming now counts only the upstream 28% (≈18g/hr) per IEA (2020).
AI use ↔ home electricityNo overlap. Inference runs in off-site data centres, so it is charged at US-average grid rates and is independent of both the household kWh slider and the selected home grid.
Grid air quality ↔ healthcare system burdenNo overlap. The $/kWh air-quality rate covers only health damages attributable to generation. The broader healthcare system burden (~$1,500/person/yr) is excluded from the total entirely — see What's Not Included.
Food health stream ↔ “Human Health Costs” harm categoryNo overlap. The harm categories are a re-slicing of the same dollars, not an additional layer: the eight harm categories sum to exactly the headline total, which is why they always show 100%.
Vehicle climate ↔ vehicle air qualityNo overlap. Distinct pathways (CO₂ warming vs. PM2.5/NOx exposure), valued from separate literatures (EPA SCC vs. NAS/APEEP).
Diet waste multiplier ↔ separate food-waste externalityNo overlap. The multiplier scales the production externality of food that is grown and then wasted; the separate term (up to $220/yr) covers disposal — landfill methane. Different lifecycle stages.
Home heating ↔ grid electricityNo overlap. Heat pump and resistive-electric heating are assigned zero direct externality here precisely because their energy is already counted in the kWh slider; only combustion heating carries its own term.

Known asymmetry. The spending multiplier scales general shopping and clothing, but not electronics — clothing is read as having a price/quality tier on top of the garment count you set, while electronics is counted by device count alone. This is a simplification rather than a principled distinction, and it means clothing responds to the spending slider while electronics does not.

Overlaps between published studies remain the hardest residual risk. Damage estimates from FAO, NAS, Epstein et al. and True Price were produced with different system boundaries, and where two sources might both include, say, agricultural water pollution, no reconciliation across their underlying accounting is possible from the published figures alone. The categories above are drawn to minimise this, but it cannot be eliminated — which is a further reason to read the total as an estimate with a wide range rather than a settled figure.
